Understanding Back Pain
     

Why Does Back Pain Happen?

     

Back pain occurs when structures supporting your spine — bones, discs, muscles, ligaments or nerves — are damaged, compressed, inflamed or worn down. It is the #1 reason for disability worldwide.

     

The spine has 33 vertebrae, 23 discs, hundreds of ligaments and a vast nerve network. A problem anywhere in this chain can trigger pain ranging from mild discomfort to debilitating agony.

     
       
✓
Disc-related problemsHerniated, bulging or degenerated discs press on nearby nerves causing pain, numbness and weakness.
       
✓
Muscle & ligament injuriesSprains, strains and spasms from poor posture, overuse or sudden awkward movements.
       
✓
Nerve compressionBone spurs, disc herniations or narrowed spinal canals squeeze nerves, causing radiating pain.
       
✓
Inflammatory conditionsConditions like ankylosing spondylitis cause progressive spine inflammation and stiffness.
       
✓
Age-related degenerationDiscs lose water content and height over time, reducing their ability to cushion the vertebrae.
     
   
 
 
   
     
🦴 Spine Anatomy
     

Understanding Your Spine's Anatomy

     

The spine is divided into four regions, each serving a specific function. Problems in any region cause different types of pain.

     
       
         
Cervical Spine
Neck — supports head, enables rotation
         
C1–C7
       
       
         
Thoracic Spine
Mid-back — connects to rib cage
         
T1–T12
       
       
         
Lumbar Spine
Lower back — most common pain source
         
L1–L5
       
       
         
Sacrum & Coccyx
Connects to pelvis, base of spine
         
S1–S5
       
     
   
   
     

Vertebrae

33 stacked bones protecting the spinal cord. Each has a central canal through which the cord passes.

     

Spinal Cord

Main nerve highway carrying signals between your brain and body. Runs from brainstem to L1/L2.

     

Intervertebral Discs

Gel-filled cushions between vertebrae acting as shock absorbers. Prone to herniation and degeneration.

     

Nerve Roots

Branches off the spinal cord exiting through small gaps. Compression causes radiculopathy (sciatica).

     

Facet Joints

Small joints at the back of each vertebra allowing movement. Arthritis here causes facet joint pain.

     

Muscles & Ligaments

Support system holding the spine upright. Sprains and spasms are among the most common pain causes.

     

Spinal Canal

The hollow channel housing the spinal cord. Narrowing (stenosis) compresses nerves, causing leg pain.

     

Sacroiliac Joints

Connect spine to pelvis. Often overlooked source of lower back and buttock pain.

   
 
 
   
Types of Back Pain
   

How Is Back Pain Classified?

   

Understanding which type of back pain you have is the first step to choosing the right treatment.

   
     
       
       

Acute Back Pain

       
Under 4 weeks
       

Comes on suddenly after injury or strain. Usually heals with rest, medication and physiotherapy. Most people recover completely.

       
             
  • Muscle strain or ligament sprain
    •          
  • Sudden injury or fall
    •          
  • Lifting something too heavy
    •          
  • Sports-related injury
    •        
     
     
       
       

Subacute Back Pain

       
4–12 weeks
       

Pain persisting beyond 4 weeks. Requires structured physiotherapy, lifestyle changes, sometimes medication. Risk of becoming chronic if untreated.

       
             
  • Partially healed disc injury
    •          
  • Ongoing muscle dysfunction
    •          
  • Poor posture habits
    •          
  • Inadequate treatment of acute pain
    •        
     
     
       
       

Chronic Back Pain

       
More than 12 weeks
       

Daily pain for 3+ months significantly affecting quality of life. Requires comprehensive multidisciplinary treatment — physical, psychological and medical.

       
             
  • Disc degeneration or herniation
    •          
  • Spinal stenosis or arthritis
    •          
  • Failed previous treatment
    •          
  • Psychological and lifestyle factors
    •        
     
   
   
     
       
       

Non-Specific Back Pain (90% of cases)

       

No specific structural cause identified. Often linked to poor posture, muscle weakness, stress and lifestyle. Responds well to physiotherapy and lifestyle changes.

     
     
       
       

Specific Back Pain (10% of cases)

       

Caused by an identifiable condition — disc herniation, fracture, infection, tumour or inflammatory disease. Requires targeted investigation and tailored treatment.

     
   
 
   
                                 
 
   
Root Causes
   

What Causes Back Pain?

   

Back pain rarely has a single cause. Multiple factors often interact. Here are the most common medical and lifestyle causes.

   
     
01

Herniated / Slip Disc

The soft inner core bulges or ruptures through the tough outer ring, pressing on nearby nerves causing severe radiating pain.

     
02

Muscle & Ligament Strain

Sudden or awkward movements or heavy lifting can sprain spinal ligaments or strain back muscles, leading to painful spasms.

     
03

Degenerative Disc Disease

Ageing causes discs to lose height, hydration and flexibility. They can no longer properly cushion vertebrae, leading to chronic pain.

     
04

Spinal Stenosis

Narrowing of the spinal canal compresses the spinal cord and nerve roots, causing leg pain, weakness and difficulty walking.

     
05

Spondylolisthesis

One vertebra slips forward over the one below, stressing nerves and muscles, often causing chronic lower back and leg pain.

     
06

Osteoarthritis (Spondylosis)

Cartilage protecting facet joints wears down with age. Bone rubbing on bone and bone spur formation cause stiffness and pain.

     
07

Ankylosing Spondylitis

An inflammatory arthritis of the spine causing chronic pain and progressive stiffness, often starting in young adults under 40.

     
08

Sciatica

Compression of the sciatic nerve — usually by a herniated disc or bone spur — causes pain radiating from the lower back down the leg.

     
09

Poor Posture & Sedentary Life

Hours of sitting with poor ergonomics places abnormal loads on spinal discs and muscles, accelerating wear and tear.

     
10

Osteoporosis

Thinning bones may fracture under everyday stress, causing sudden severe pain — especially in older women.

     
11

Scoliosis

Abnormal lateral curvature of the spine places uneven stress on muscles, discs and joints, causing chronic pain and postural imbalance.

     
12

Referred Pain from Organs

Kidney stones, infections, endometriosis and aortic aneurysm can all cause back pain that mimics musculoskeletal conditions.

   
 
 
   
👤 Who Is at Risk?
   

Risk Factors for Back Pain

   

These factors increase your chance of developing chronic back pain. Knowing them helps you take preventive action.

   
     

Age (Over 45)

Disc degeneration, arthritis and bone density loss accelerate after 40–45. Back pain becomes more common and severe with each decade.

     

Obesity & Being Overweight

Every extra kg adds compressive load on the lumbar spine. Obesity strongly linked to disc degeneration, osteoarthritis and chronic pain.

     

Sedentary / Desk-job Lifestyle

Sitting for 8+ hours daily weakens core muscles and places 40% more pressure on lumbar discs than standing.

     

Heavy Physical Work

Occupations requiring repetitive lifting, bending, twisting or whole-body vibration significantly raise injury risk.

     

Smoking

Nicotine reduces blood flow to discs, accelerating degeneration. Smokers have 1.5× higher risk of chronic back pain.

     

Psychological Stress & Anxiety

Chronic stress causes muscle tension. Depression and anxiety are strongly associated with severity and chronicity of back pain.

     

Genetics / Family History

Disc degeneration and ankylosing spondylitis have genetic components. Parents with chronic back pain raises your risk.

     

Pregnancy

Hormonal changes loosen ligaments, while the growing uterus shifts the centre of gravity placing extra strain on the lumbar spine.

   
 
 
   
     
🚨 Red Flag Warning Signs
     

When Should You See a Spine Specialist?

     

Most back pain improves in 2–4 weeks. However, certain symptoms require immediate medical attention. Never ignore these red flags:

     
       
<

Loss of Bladder/Bowel Control

Go to the ER immediately. May indicate cauda equina syndrome — a surgical emergency.

       
<

Pain After a Fall or Accident

Needs urgent imaging to rule out fractures, dislocation or spinal cord injury.

       
<

Weakness in Legs

Progressive weakness or foot drop indicates serious nerve compression requiring urgent evaluation.

       
<

Back Pain with Fever

May indicate spinal infection (discitis, epidural abscess) — a potentially life-threatening condition.

       
<

Unexplained Weight Loss

Back pain with unintentional weight loss needs cancer screening. Spinal tumours must be ruled out.

       
<

Severe Nighttime Pain

Pain worst at night that doesn't improve with any position change is a red flag for infection or malignancy.

       
<

Pain Lasting Over 6 Weeks

Any back pain lasting 6+ weeks without improvement despite rest and OTC medications needs evaluation.

       
<

Severe Pain Not Responding to Meds

Pain preventing normal activity and unresponsive to standard painkillers warrants urgent assessment.

💊 Advanced Treatment
   

Treatment Options for Back Pain

   

Treatment is tailored to the type, severity and duration of your pain. We always start with the least invasive approach.

   
                           
   
     

Exercise Therapy

Targeted strengthening of core, back extensor and hip muscles. Includes McKenzie exercises, Pilates-based rehab and graded activity programs.

     

Manual Therapy

Hands-on mobilisation and manipulation of the spine and surrounding joints to restore movement, reduce stiffness and relieve acute pain.

     

TENS & Electrotherapy

Electrical stimulation modalities including TENS, interferential therapy and ultrasound to reduce pain and promote tissue healing.

     

Posture & Ergonomics Training

Education on correct sitting, standing, lifting and sleeping positions to reduce spinal load and prevent recurrence.

     

Heat & Cold Therapy

Targeted heat relaxes muscle spasms; cold reduces inflammation and acute pain. Both used strategically by our physios.

     

Dry Needling / IMS

Fine needles inserted into trigger points to release chronic spasms and myofascial pain. Highly effective for long-standing muscular back pain.

   
   
     

Anti-inflammatory Medications (NSAIDs)

Medications like ibuprofen and naproxen reduce inflammation and pain. Used short-term for acute flare-ups.

     

Muscle Relaxants

Prescribed for severe muscle spasms accompanying acute back pain. Used for short durations due to side-effect profiles.

     

Neuropathic Pain Medications

Medications like pregabalin and duloxetine address nerve pain components, especially useful in sciatica and chronic radiculopathy.

     

Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)

Structured psychological therapy addressing pain catastrophising, fear-avoidance and mood disorders that perpetuate chronic back pain.

   
   
     

Epidural Steroid Injection

Corticosteroid injected into the epidural space to dramatically reduce inflammation and nerve pain. Relief lasting weeks to months.

     

Facet Joint Injection

Steroid injected directly into the facet joint to treat arthritis-related joint pain. Combined with medial branch nerve blocks for diagnosis and treatment.

     

Radiofrequency Ablation (RFA)

Heat used to deactivate nerves supplying painful facet joints, providing long-lasting pain relief for 6 months to 2 years.

     

PRP / Regenerative Injections

Platelet-Rich Plasma injections harness the body's own growth factors to promote healing of damaged discs, ligaments and tendons.

   
   
     

Microdiscectomy

Minimally invasive removal of the herniated disc fragment pressing on the nerve. Keyhole surgery, small scar, fast recovery. Most common spine surgery for sciatica.

     

Endoscopic Spine Surgery

Ultra-minimally invasive surgery through a tiny tube with a camera. Minimal blood loss, same-day discharge possible, fastest recovery of all surgical options.

     

Spinal Fusion

Two or more vertebrae are permanently joined with bone graft and hardware to stabilise the spine. Used for spondylolisthesis, severe degeneration and fractures.

     

Laminectomy / Decompression

The lamina (roof of the vertebra) is removed to create space and relieve pressure on nerves. Primary treatment for spinal stenosis causing severe leg pain.

     

Artificial Disc Replacement

The damaged disc is replaced with an artificial implant that preserves spine motion. Alternative to fusion that maintains flexibility at the treated level.

     

Spinal Cord Stimulation

A device implanted near the spinal cord sends mild electrical pulses to mask pain signals. For patients with chronic pain not responding to other treatments.

🌿 Prevention
   

How to Prevent Back Pain

   

Many back pain episodes are preventable. These evidence-based strategies protect your spine for the long term.

   
     

Strengthen Your Core

Strong abdominal and back muscles act as a natural corset for the spine. Daily core exercises reduce back pain risk by up to 60%.

     

Stay Active

Regular low-impact exercise — walking, swimming, cycling — keeps discs hydrated and muscles healthy. Even 30 minutes daily makes a huge difference.

     

Fix Your Posture

Sit with your back straight, feet flat on the floor, screen at eye level. Use a lumbar support cushion for long desk sessions.

     

Lift Correctly

Always bend at the knees, not the waist, when lifting. Keep loads close to your body and never twist while carrying heavy items.

     

Maintain Healthy Weight

Every 5 kg of extra weight adds significant compressive load to the lumbar spine. Weight loss relieves back pain in overweight patients.

     

Sleep Right

Sleep on your side with a pillow between your knees, or on your back with a pillow under your knees. Avoid sleeping on your stomach.

     

Quit Smoking

Smoking restricts blood flow to discs, accelerating degeneration. Quitting significantly slows the progression of disc disease.

     

Manage Stress

Chronic stress causes persistent muscle tension in the back and neck. Yoga, meditation or regular deep breathing reduce musculoskeletal pain.

❓ FAQ
   

Frequently Asked Questions

   

Answers to the questions our patients ask most often.

   
     
       
Can back pain be cured without surgery?
▾
       
Yes — over 90% of back pain cases, including most slip disc and sciatica cases, can be treated successfully without surgery using physiotherapy, medication, lifestyle changes and/or injections. Surgery is recommended only when conservative treatment fails after a sufficient trial, or when there are neurological red flags like progressive weakness or bladder/bowel symptoms.
     
     
       
How do I know if my back pain is serious?
▾
       
Most back pain is mechanical and not dangerous. However, seek immediate care if you have: loss of bladder or bowel control, pain after a trauma, weakness in the legs, back pain with fever, or pain that is progressively worsening despite rest. Pain lasting more than 6 weeks without improvement also needs evaluation.
     
     
       
How long does back pain treatment take?
▾
       
Acute back pain typically resolves in 2–6 weeks with appropriate treatment. Subacute pain takes 6–12 weeks. Chronic back pain may require 3–6 months of structured treatment for meaningful improvement. Surgical patients often return to daily activities within 2–6 weeks depending on the procedure. We set realistic expectations at your first consultation.
     
     
       
Is physiotherapy effective for slip disc / sciatica?
▾
       
Yes. Physiotherapy is a cornerstone of slip disc and sciatica treatment. Specific exercises like the McKenzie method reduce disc bulge, take pressure off nerves and strengthen the muscles supporting the spine. Most patients see significant improvement within 4–8 weeks of consistent physiotherapy.
     
     
       
What is minimally invasive spine surgery and is it safe?
▾
       
Minimally invasive spine surgery (MISS) uses small keyhole incisions instead of large open cuts. Advantages include: less blood loss, lower infection risk, less post-operative pain, shorter hospital stay (often 1–2 days) and faster return to normal activities. In experienced hands, it is extremely safe and produces results equivalent or superior to open surgery.

Does insurance cover spine treatment?
▾
       
Most health insurance policies cover spine surgery, advanced imaging (MRI/CT), and hospital stays. Physiotherapy and injection-based treatments may have partial coverage depending on your policy. Our billing team will verify your insurance benefits before your treatment starts and guide you through the claims process.
     
     
       
I had spine surgery elsewhere and still have pain — can you help?
▾
       
Absolutely. Failed Back Surgery Syndrome (FBSS) — persistent or recurrent pain after spine surgery — is a recognised condition we treat regularly. We review your previous surgery records and imaging, identify the specific pain generator, and offer targeted solutions including revision surgery, spinal cord stimulation, or comprehensive pain management programs.
